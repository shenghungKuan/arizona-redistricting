## 1. Introduction

Every ten years, following the completion of the U.S. Census, each state is required to redraw its legislative and congressional district boundaries in a process known as redistricting. This ensures that districts remain roughly equal in population, reflecting shifts and growth in the population over the previous decade. Redistricting is crucial for maintaining fair and effective representation, as it affects how communities are grouped for elections and which voters politicians are accountable to. The process can vary by state—some entrust it to independent commissions, while others allow state legislatures to draw the lines—but it always plays a significant role in shaping political power for the next ten years.

The process of redistricting can be subject to tampering through a practice called gerrymandering, where district lines are intentionally drawn to benefit a particular political party or group. Political gerrymandering involves shaping districts to favor one party, often resulting in oddly shaped boundaries that dilute opposition votes. Racial gerrymandering, on the other hand, involves drawing lines to weaken the voting power of racial or ethnic minorities, often in violation of the Voting Rights Act. Both forms can undermine democratic representation and fairness, making the redistricting process a critical and often contentious part of American democracy.

This paper presents an analysis of the Arizona legislative district map, focusing on its implications for the Democratic Party and Native American communities. By examining the district boundaries through these lenses, the analysis aims to assess how effectively the current map supports fair representation and whether it reflects—or distorts—the political and demographic realities of the state.

### 2.4 District History

#### 2.4.1 History of Discrimination

Arizona was placed under the preclearance provisions of the Voting Rights Act in 1975 due to a history of racial discrimination in voting, this law has been historically leveraged in Arizona lawsuits against enacted district maps.

Under preclearance, Arizona and eight other mainly Southern states had to obtain approval from the U.S. Department of Justice for changes to election laws affecting racial minorities. From 1982, the DOJ blocked four statewide redistricting plans in Arizona for being discriminatory. Arizona's first two independent redistricting commissions were subject to preclearance. 

In June 2013, the U.S. Supreme Court invalidated the preclearance provisions in Shelby County v. Holder (5-4 ruling). Although federal protections for minority voting rights still exist, enforcement has become more difficult in Arizona since preclearance was removed.

#### 2.4.2 Redistricting in the 1990's

There was a 40% increase in population in the state of Arizona from 1990 to 2000's (U.S. Census Bureau. 2001). A significant change in a state's population will require additional congressional and (or) state legislative district boundaries to be drawn. Such a population change is also a potential indicator for a population socio-economic demographics to shift as well as political ones. Arizona would gain one more congressional district in the 90's going from five to six congressional districts.

In the 90's the Arizona State Legislature was responsible for drawing the state's congressional and legislative district maps. This process was controlled by the majority party in the legislature. Legislators were reluctant to drastically change district boundaries from which they found support in, and the state legislature would often make decisions that would protect incumbents.

Arizona had a significant rise in lawsuits against the State, that showed the shift and level awareness of fair representation among public community groups where redistricting is concerned:

**Arizonans for Fair Representation v. Symington**, 828 F. Supp. 684 (D. Ariz. 1992)

Although the legislative senate district map had been agreed upon by the Arizona Senate (Majority Democrat) and the Arizona House of Representatives (Majority Republican), they found themselves in
an impasse when it came to the congressional district map. The Court determined that the plan selected must meet three basic criteria: (1) the Constitution, (2) the Voting Rights Act (3) "neutral principles of redistricting" The Court further described the neutral criteria as preserving communities of interest, providing compact and contiguous districts, and protection of incumbents. 

The Court ultimately rejected the Senate and House plans in favor of a Court drafted plan inspired by intervenor Indian tribes. The court also found no evidence of political polarization, but the Court Map was superior in three basic criteria. This is meaningful for the state because the office that is responsible for drafting the map, in a manner of speaking lost that privilege, and it opens the door for discussions on the office that is responsible for drafting plans.

**Arizona State Senate v. Arizonans for Fair Representation**, 507 U.S. 980 (1993) (mem.)

The Arizona State Senate appealed the decision in Arizonans for Fair Representation v. Symington on three substantive grounds: (1) First, the U.S. District Court plan caused a retrogression of minority voting strength because Arizona had gained a congressional seat (from five to six) and gained in minority population, but the Court created only one majority-minority district when it could have created two. (2) The Senate argued that the lower court erred in finding that there was insufficient evidence of polarized voting and, therefore, no remedial action was required under the Voting Rights Act. (3) the Senate argued that the District Court failed to comply with the requirements of the Voting Rights Act by improperly considering "neutral" redistricting factors over minority voting rights. 

Although the lower court hearing was upheld, and the Court Ruled Map remained, the case shows the growing tension in the state between Race-conscious redistricting & traditional redistricting principles. During the pendency of the appeal, Republicans gained the majority in the Senate and had the appeal dismissed.

**Hispanic Chamber of Commerce v. Arizonans for Fair Representation**, 507 U.S. 981(1993) (mem.)

Intervenors & the Hispanic Chamber of Commerce, appealed Arizonans for Fair Representation v. Symington on several grounds: (1) The Hispanic Chamber of Commerce claimed in its appellants' brief that the District Court erred in giving "good government" criteria primacy over minority voting rights.
(2) The Chamber also argued that the lower court should have found on the evidence that racially polarized voting does exist in Arizona and that the Court erred in ordering its plan into effect until 2000 without preclearance under the Voting Rights Act. The lower court judgment was summarily affirmed by the United States Supreme Court, and the Court's Drafted plan remained.

The case reflects a judicial tendency in the early 1990s to limit race-based redistricting mandates unless strong, clear evidence showed legal violations. Highlighting the challenges communities faced in electoral influence through litigation, especially when courts demanded strong statistical and political evidence to justify new majority-minority districts.


#### 2.4.3 Redistricting in the 2000's & Proposition 106

Efforts to remove redistricting authority from the state legislature that began in the 90's continued in the 2000's, when Arizona Common Cause, the League of Women Voters, and the Valley Citizens League joined forces to create the Fair Districts Fair Elections Committee. Their goal was to place the Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (AIRC) proposal on the ballot for the November 2000 election. Reform advocates hoped that the prospect of a ballot initiative would encourage the Legislature to present a suitable alternative plan to voters—but the Legislature failed to do so. (Berman, David R. Arizona Redistricting: A Perspective on the Process. Morrison Institute for Public Policy, Arizona State University, Apr. 2022)

In November 2000, Arizona voters passed Proposition 106, a citizen initiative that amended the Arizona Constitution by removing the power to draw congressional and state legislative districts from the state legislature and reassigning this task to the Independent Redistricting Commission (IRC).

#### **Navajo Nation v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (2002)**

The Navajo Nation challenged the legislative redistricting plan adopted by the newly formed Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (AIRC), arguing it violated the Voting Rights Act by diluting Native American voting strength. 

This was one of the first major legal challenges to the Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (AIRC) after it was created by Proposition 106. It signaled that even an independent commission must still comply with federal protections like the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which prohibits vote dilution of racial or language minority groups. Clarifying the responsibilities of independent commissions in protecting minority voting rights. 

This case brought greater visibility to how redistricting affects tribal nations, whose electoral needs often differ from other racial and ethnic minorities. Which laid groundwork for ongoing litigation and advocacy around Native American representation in both state and federal redistricting processes, not just in Arizona but nationally.

Ultimately a federal court ordered the use of a revised interim plan for the 2002 elections, with members elected serving full terms, it did not fully reject the IRC's plan but gave they time to work on an improved map.

#### **Arizona Minority Coalition for Fair Redistricting v. AIRC & Ricarte v. AIRC (2004)**

These consolidated cases alleged that the AIRC's legislative and congressional redistricting plans failed to favor competitive districts, as required by the Arizona Constitution. The trial court initially found the legislative plan unconstitutional for not adequately considering competitiveness. However, the Arizona Court of Appeals later reversed this decision, upholding the commission's plans.

This case clarified that competitiveness is important but not absolute, it is one factor among many in Arizona’s redistricting process. The ruling effectively gave the AIRC broad discretion to balance multiple goals and affirmed the Commission’s independence in making difficult trade-offs. As opposed to the 90's were protection for the incumbent and competitiveness outweighed other factors.

#### **Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (2015)**

The Arizona State Legislature sued the AIRC, claiming that the commission's authority to draw congressional districts violated the U.S. Constitution’s Elections Clause (“The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof…”) meant only the elected legislature could draw congressional districts. The Supreme Court rejected that narrow interpretation, ruling that “Legislature” can also mean the legislative power of the people, including ballot initiatives. This interpretation empowered citizens to enact electoral reforms through direct democracy.

The case became a cornerstone for future redistricting reform efforts nationwide. It gave legal cover and confidence to states considering independent or citizen-led commissions to combat gerrymandering and promote fair representation.

#### Harris v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (2016)

Plaintiffs challenged the AIRC's legislative redistricting plan, arguing that population deviations among districts violated the "one person, one vote" principle. The Court ruled 9–0 that the minor deviations (under 10%) were allowable—especially when made to meet Voting Rights Act (VRA) obligations and protect minority voting power. This upheld the idea that mathematical precision is not required if the deviations serve legitimate state interests.

The U.S. Supreme Court unanimously upheld the plan, finding that the deviations were minor and resulted from the commission's efforts to comply with the Voting Rights Act. This case set a precedent for other states, showing that modest population variances are defensible if they are part of a broader effort to ensure racial equity and compliance with federal law.

## 3. Methods

This report presents an analysis of Arizona’s 2022 enacted State Legislative district map, using 2020 Census data and results from two statewide elections held in 2020. We employ Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) ensemble methods to evaluate whether the characteristics of the 2022 map align with expectations from a nonpartisan redistricting process or if they represent statistical outliers. Marginal box plots are used to visualize patterns that may indicate partisan gerrymandering. In addition, Short Burst analyses are conducted to assess the distribution and representational impact on Native American Age Population (NAVAP) with particular attention to Democratic voters.

### 3.1 Data Collection and Preparation

Voting Age Population data was downloaded from Redistricting Data Hub [Arizona block PL 94-171 2020](https://redistrictingdatahub.org/dataset/arizona-block-pl-94171-2020-by-table/), as well as County Line Data [Arizona county PL 94-171 2020](https://redistrictingdatahub.org/dataset/arizona-county-pl-94171-2020/) on March 30, 2025. The Approved Senate Legislative District Map was downloaded from Redistricting Data Hub [2022 Arizona State Legislature Districts Approved Plan](https://redistrictingdatahub.org/dataset/2022-arizona-state-legislature-districts-approved-plan/). The 2020 Arizona Presidential and Senate election results were downloaded from Redistricting Hub via the Vest Team [2022 Election Results](https://redistrictingdatahub.org/dataset/vest-2020-arizona-precinct-and-election-results/)

The collected datasets were analyzed and fixed by maup library. All datasets were converted to the same CRS: 4269 and converted into the same geometry units. The combined dataset was re-analyzed by maup.doctor(), and the analysis result is ”True”. Any unused data was removed from the final data set. The final dataset  was exported as a shape and .geojson file.

### 3.2 Ensemble Analysis

The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method is a powerful algorithm for generating samples from a probability distribution, with the goal of approximating an equilibrium distribution. GerryChain, a Python library developed specifically for redistricting analysis, leverages advanced MCMC techniques to explore the vast space of possible redistricting configurations, and is leveraged to find an equilibrium distribution that represents a neutral ensemble of districting plans. A key feature of GerryChain and configuration for the Markov chain is the ReCom (recursive merge-and-split) algorithm, which creates new districts by merging neighboring districts and then splitting them in a way that maintains essential criteria such as contiguity and equal population.

In this analysis, we have configured our Markov chain with the following parameters:  
Chain Length: 40,000 steps.  
Initial Plan: 2022 Enacted District Map. 
Elections: The Presidential election of 2020 (PRE) and U.S. Senate 2020 (USS) election were primarily analyzed.

Ensembles used in this Analysis:  

1. Efficiency Gap: Measures wasted votes of one party, compared with the other party.
2. Democratic Won: Tracks the number of districts won by the Democratic party
3. Cutting Edge: Evaluates the number of edges cut in the graph representation of
districts, and shows the compactness of districts.

### 3.3 Marginal Box Plots

The marginal box plots—often referred to as the signature of gerrymandering, illustrates how votes are distributed across all districts and can indicate whether this distribution has been skewed to benefit a particular political party. In this report, the box plots display the percentage of Democratic votes in each of Arizona’s State Senate Legislative  districts. The districts are ordered from the lowest to the highest percentage of Democratic support to highlight patterns of potential partisan advantage. The analysis is based on the 2022 enacted map, using vote data from the Presidential (PRE) and U.S. Senate (USS) elections

### 3.4 Short Burst Analysis

Short Burst analysis is a technique used within ensemble methods to assess the potential for creating majority-minority districts for a specific population group. By running a Markov Chain and selecting an outlier plan after a defined number of steps—known as the burst length, this method identifies the maximum number of districts in which a minority group can form a majority. It is commonly applied to evaluate compliance with the Voting Rights Act (VRA) and to explore the representational potential of historically underrepresented populations.

In this report, we performed Short Burst analysis using two different burst lengths 10 and 50. The figures presented highlight the results from the burst length that yielded the greatest number of majority-minority districts. This analysis was applied specifically to the Native American Voting Age Population (AMINVAP) to assess their potential for majority representation.

In this section, we will present the results from the Outlier Analysis, Marginal box plots, and the Short Burst Analysis, comparing the 2022 State Senate Legislative map to the ensembles generated by Monte Carlo Markov Chain.

### 4.1 Outlier Analysis - Cutting Edges

In Figure 1, we compare the number of cut edges in the 2022 State Legislative map to those from an MCMC ensemble. A cut edge refers to a boundary where two adjacent geographic units (such as precincts or blocks) belong to different districts. Essentially, a measure of how “choppy” or irregular district borders are. The ensemble histogram shows a mean of approximately 825 cut edges, while the 2022 map contains about 869. Although the enacted map falls within two standard deviations of the ensemble mean, indicating a slightly higher number of boundary cuts, the difference is not significant. Overall, the number of cut edges in the 2022 plan is reasonably close to the ensemble average and does not raise concerns about unusually irregular district shapes.

### 4.3 Marginal Box Plots

Figure 7 and 8 presents box plots for the Democratic vote percentages by district for the 2022 redistricting plan, plotted against an ensemble distribution of each district from the Presidential election and Senate election in 2020.
