## Introduction

### What is a Census Block, Precinct, & District?

#### **Census Block**

A census block is the smallest unit of geography used by the U.S. Census Bureau. Think of it like the building block of all other areas.

#### **Precinct**

A precinct is the smallest political unit where elections are administered. It’s where you’re assigned to go vote. Multiple precincts make up a district

#### District 

A **district** is a large geographic area used to elect a representative. Think of it as a voting zone for a specific level of government.

### What is a District Map?

A district map is a map that shows how a geographic area—like a state, county, or city—is divided into political districts. These districts determine which voters are grouped together for the purpose of electing representatives to legislative bodies (like Congress, state legislatures, or city councils).

A quick look at the **Arizona Congressional District Map**

- Is used to elect U.S. House of Representatives members (Arizona has 9 congressional districts as of the 2020 Census).
- Each congressional district elects one representative to Congress.
- These districts must have roughly equal populations, based on total population from the Census.

A quick look at the **Arizona State Legislative District Map**

- Is used to elect State Senator and State House Representative in elections, ensuring fair and equal representation across the state. (Arizona has 30 State Senate districts as of the 2020 Census).
- Each state legislative district elects one senator and two state house representatives.
- As of the 2020 Census, each of Arizona's 30 legislative districts has a population of about 230,000 residents.

### What is Redistricting

Redistricting is the process of redrawing the boundaries of electoral districts — the geographic areas used to elect representatives to legislative bodies like Congress, state legislatures, city councils, etc.

Redistricting usually happens every 10 years after the census to reflect changes in population. The goal is to ensure equal representation, meaning each district should have roughly the same number of people.

### What is Gerrymandering

Is when the process of redistricting is manipulated to give an unfair advantage to a particular political party, group, or incumbent.

It usually results in oddly-shaped districts that don’t make geographic or community sense. Instead, they’re drawn to:

- **Pack** opponents into a few districts (so they win fewer overall)
- **Crack** opponents across many districts (so they don't form a majority anywhere)

### District History

#### History of Discrimination

Arizona was placed under the preclearance provisions of the Voting Rights Act in 1975 due to a history of racial discrimination in voting, this law has been historically leveraged in Arizona lawsuits against enacted district maps.
- Under preclearance, Arizona and eight other mainly Southern states had to obtain approval from the U.S. Department of Justice for changes to election laws affecting racial minorities.

- From 1982, the DOJ blocked four statewide redistricting plans in Arizona for being discriminatory.

- Arizona's first two independent redistricting commissions were subject to preclearance.

- In June 2013, the U.S. Supreme Court invalidated the preclearance provisions in Shelby County v. Holder (5-4 ruling).

Although federal protections for minority voting rights still exist, enforcement has become more difficult in Arizona since preclearance was removed.

#### Redistricting in the 1990's
There was a 40% increase in population in the state of Arizona from 1990 to 2000's (U.S. Census Bureau. 2001). A significant change in a state's population
will require additional congressional and state legislative district boundaries to be drawn. Such a population change is also a potential indicator for a population socio-economic demographics
to shift as well as political ones.

Prior to 2001 the Arizona State Legislature was responsible for drawing the state's congressional and legislative district maps. This process was
controlled by the majority party in the legislature. Legislators were reluctant to drastically change district boundaries from which they found support in
and main goal was to protect incumbents.

The 1990's in Arizona had some significant lawsuits that showed the shift and level awareness of public community groups where redistricting is concerned:

**Arizonans for Fair Representation v. Symington**, 828 F. Supp. 684 (D. Ariz. 1992)

Although the legislative senate district map had been agreed upon by the Arizona Senate (Majority Democrat) and the Arizona House of Representatives (Majority Republican), they found themselves in
an impasse when it came to the congressional district map. The Court determined that the plan selected must meet three basic criteria:
- the Constitution,
- the Voting Rights Act
- and "neutral principles of redistricting"

The Court further described the neutral criteria as preserving communities of interest, providing compact and contiguous districts, and protection of incumbents. 
The Court ultimately rejected the Senate and House plans in favor of a Court drafted plan inspired by intervenor Indian tribes. The court also found no evidence of 
political polarization. 

**Arizona State Senate v. Arizonans for Fair Representation**, 507 U.S. 980 (1993) (mem.)
The Arizona State Senate appealed the decision in Arizonans for Fair Representation v. Symington on three substantive grounds:
- First, the U.S. District Court plan caused a retrogression of minority voting strength because Arizona had gained a congressional seat (from five to six) and gained in minority population, but the Court created only one majority-minority district when it could have created two.
- Second, the Senate argued that the lower court erred in finding that there was insufficient evidence of polarized voting and, therefore, no remedial action was required under the Voting Rights Act.
- Finally, the Senate argued that the District Court failed to comply with the requirements of the Voting Rights Act by improperly considering "neutral" redistricting factors over minority voting rights. 
During the pendency of the appeal, Republicans gained the majority in the Senate and had the appeal dismissed.

**Hispanic Chamber of Commerce v. Arizonans for Fair Representation**, 507 U.S. 981(1993) (mem.)
Intervenors, Hispanic Chamber of Commerce, appealed Arizonans for Fair Representation v. Symington on several grounds:
- The Hispanic Chamber of Commerce claimed in its appellants' brief that the District Court erred in giving "good government" criteria primacy over minority voting rights.
- The Chamber also argued that the lower court should have found on the evidence that racially polarized voting does exist in Arizona and that the Court erred in ordering its plan into effect until 2000 without preclearance under the Voting Rights Act.

The lower court judgment was summarily affirmed by the United States Supreme Court.


#### Redistricting in the 2000's & Proposition 106

"Organized efforts to take the redistricting
task out of the hands of legislators began
in 1999 when Arizona Common Cause, the
League of Women Voters and the Valley
Citizens League formed the Fair Districts Fair
Elections Committee to put the AIRC proposal
on the November 2000 ballot. Reformers
hoped that the threat of an initiative would
prompt the Legislature to submit a suitable
plan to the voters, but the Legislature failed to
do so." (Berman, David R. Arizona Redistricting: A Perspective on the Process. Morrison Institute for Public Policy, Arizona State University, Apr. 2022)

In November 2000, Arizona voters passed Proposition 106, a citizen initiative that amended the Arizona Constitution by removing the power to draw congressional and state legislative districts from the state legislature and reassigning this task to the Independent Redistricting Commission (IRC)
(_AIRC_, https://irc.az.gov/about/proposition-106. Accessed 20 Apr. 2025.).

<figure>
    <img width="409" height="238" src="/report/images/prop_106_election.png"
         alt="proposition 106 ballot">
    <figcaption>Proposition 106 election results, "yes" supported creating a 5 member independent Redistricting Commission to draw legislative and congressional district boundaries</figcaption>
</figure>

Under the provisions of Proposition 106,
as found in the Arizona Constitution, each
decade, the majority and minority leaders of
each chamber of the Legislature select four
of the five commission members from a list of
25 candidates (10 from each major party, five
who do not belong to a major party) that has
been compiled and vetted by the Commission
of Appellate Court Appointments (CAC). The
four members then select the fifth member by
majority vote, and that person acts as chair of
the commission. The fifth commissioner — the 
chair — cannot belong to the same political
party as any other commission member. They
must register as “unaffiliated with a political
party for three or more years immediately
preceding appointment.”

The proposition mandates:

- Consider where incumbents or candidates for office reside when drawing districts
- To start from scratch with a grid map that ignores incumbency and political data.
- It brings legislative and party leaders into the choice of
commissioners and encourages an outcome
where we have two Republican commissioners,
two Democratic commissioners, and one
independent commissioner who acts as a swing on important questions
- when drawing districts:
  - Roughly equal in population
  - Protect the voting strength of minorities
  - Geographically Compact, Contiguous, and reflect communities of interest.
  - Competitive Districts are favored

#### **Navajo Nation v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (2002)**

The Navajo Nation challenged the legislative redistricting plan adopted by the newly formed Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (AIRC), arguing it violated the Voting Rights Act by diluting Native American voting strength. A federal court ordered the use of a revised interim plan for the 2002 elections, with members elected serving full terms.​

#### **Arizona Minority Coalition for Fair Redistricting v. AIRC & Ricarte v. AIRC (2004)**

These consolidated cases alleged that the AIRC's legislative and congressional redistricting plans failed to favor competitive districts, as required by the Arizona Constitution. The trial court initially found the legislative plan unconstitutional for not adequately considering competitiveness. However, the Arizona Court of Appeals later reversed this decision, upholding the commission's plans.

#### **Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (2015)**
The Arizona State Legislature sued the AIRC, claiming that the commission's authority to draw congressional districts violated the U.S. Constitution's Elections Clause. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld the commission's authority, affirming that voters could establish independent commissions to draw district lines.

#### Harris v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (2016)
Plaintiffs challenged the AIRC's legislative redistricting plan, arguing that population deviations among districts violated the "one person, one vote" principle. The U.S. Supreme Court unanimously upheld the plan, finding that the deviations were minor and resulted from the commission's efforts to comply with the Voting Rights Act.​
